This blog starts from a practical problem in my research. I need to follow what parliaments are doing with AI, but new information does not arrive on a publication schedule. A short report can preserve a useful observation while the larger research questions are still being worked through.

I want those reports to be useful to other people too. That means giving readers enough detail to understand a case, follow its sources and decide whether my interpretation is convincing.

## What I want to know

The questions are fairly concrete. What task does the system support? Who uses it? Does its output become part of an official record, inform research or help someone prepare a decision? Who checks the result, and what happens when it is wrong?

A transcription tool and a tool for researching legislation may both be described as “parliamentary AI”, but they occupy different places in parliamentary work. The reports here should explain those differences without assuming that every reader is a parliamentary scholar.

## What a report can establish

An announcement tells us what an institution says it plans to do. It does not necessarily tell us whether staff use the system, whether it saves time or whether its outputs are reliable. I want to keep those distinctions visible.

Sometimes the most useful conclusion will be that a particular question remains unanswered. I would rather leave a gap in a case profile than fill it with an inference that the sources cannot support.

## A record I can return to

Over time, short reports can feed into fuller case profiles and comparisons. The blog will complement my academic work by recording developments and making preliminary observations available for discussion. It will not replace the slower work of interviewing, comparing cases and testing an argument.

I am starting small. There is no promise of daily coverage, and publication will depend on the available evidence and research time.
